**The Open Group** is a global, vendor-neutral technology consortium. It was formed in 1996 through the merger of X/Open and the Open Software Foundation, and it's known for maintaining the UNIX certification and a set of open standards. Besides TOGAF, its best-known standards are ArchiMate (a modeling language for architecture) and IT4IT (a reference architecture for managing IT).

**TOGAF** (The Open Group Architecture Framework) is its enterprise architecture framework. It began in the mid-1990s, drawing on a US Department of Defense architecture framework. Its core is the Architecture Development Method (ADM), a phased, iterative process for moving from business vision through data, application, and technology architecture to implementation and governance. It also includes an architecture repository, governance guidance, and reference models. The 10th Edition (2022) restructured the standard into a modular form so teams can adopt parts of it rather than the whole thing.

**Certification.** The current paths include TOGAF Enterprise Architecture Foundation and Practitioner, alongside a Business Architecture Foundation certification, and older TOGAF 9 credentials. The combined exam is cheaper than taking the two parts separately and has no prerequisites. Exam pricing runs roughly $400 to $600 depending on the route. Your TOGAF 9 credential remains valid, so you don't need to recertify.

**How the industry values it**

_Where it's valued:_

- Enterprise architect roles at large consulting firms such as Accenture, Deloitte, IBM, and Capgemini routinely list TOGAF as required or preferred.
- Job-posting trackers report a large number of listings referencing it, mostly for enterprise, solutions, and domain architect roles. One tracker counted over 2,600 active postings, with an upward trend through the first half of 2026.
- It's strongest in government, financial services, and large enterprises. It works as a screening credential and a shared vocabulary, particularly in client-facing consulting.
- Because it's tied to no technology vendor, it keeps its value across ecosystems, unlike platform-specific credentials.

_Where it's questioned:_

- Practitioners often criticize TOGAF as heavyweight, process-heavy, and slow, especially in agile, cloud-native, and product-oriented organizations. Many teams adopt a lightweight subset and ignore the rest.
- It's seen as a signal of knowledge, not of ability. Hiring managers for hands-on architecture roles often weigh delivery experience and cloud-provider certifications more heavily.
- It gets less attention among startups and engineering-led companies, where platform engineering, internal developer portals (like the Backstage topic we discussed earlier), and lightweight methods such as the C4 model fill similar needs.
- Even supporters note it delivers the most career value when paired with real architecture responsibility.

**A caution about the numbers.** Nearly everything I found on salary premiums and "worth it" verdicts comes from training providers and certification-prep sites that profit from selling the credential. Claims like a 15-25% pay premium or $150,000 median salaries are unverified marketing figures and vary a lot by region and role. For a more neutral read, look at job postings in your target market and ask architects at companies you'd want to work for.

**Rule of thumb:** TOGAF is worth it if you're aiming at enterprise architecture in large organizations, consulting, or government, where it's a gatekeeping credential. It's less compelling if you want to stay a hands-on engineer or work in a fast-moving product company. In that case, cloud architect certifications and demonstrable system design experience will usually carry more weight.

Are you weighing the certification for yourself, or evaluating TOGAF as a framework for your organization? The answer differs a fair bit between the two.
